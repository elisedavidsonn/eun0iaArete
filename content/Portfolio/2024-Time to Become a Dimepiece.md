# Self-Evaluation and Self-Evasion: Time to Become a Dimepiece

As the synchronous strikes of Time enclose the citizens of London upon a measured temporality, Virginia Woolf's readers are consistently reminded of the entropic narration and fallibility of authority within her "stream-of-consciousness" narrative. While this technique serves Woolf's modernist intent of figural interpretation of human experience and non-linearity, it also warrants examination as a quasi-effectual mode of (re)enforcing notions of gendered trait or thought that, over time, further essentialized unto literary historicization of epistemological knowing. The autotelic flow adopted by Woolf is not merely a stylistic innovation (as prior have contended in comparing James Joyce's "Ulysses" to Woolf's works); rather, for her, it is a radical mode of inquiry into life and death. However, in attempting to situate oneself within a framework of self-knowledge amid external social and historical forces, one faces entrenched paradigms of differential/signified thought and/or skill, and ultimately encounters lived experience transformed into rigid, hierarchical representations of authority and power predicated on "capacity." This framing produces the overarching tendency to naturalized characteristic traits along dominant sociocultural lines of sex and gender, thereby restricting individualistic expression to these 'inherited' templates. Woolf embeds these hierarchies deeply within "Mrs Dalloway's" setting, invoking the structures of post-Edwardian London and drawing from a Western philosophical and theological lineage that privileges what Jacques Derrida termed "phallogocentrism," and what Donna Haraway defines as the veneration of "the monotheistic, phallic, authoritative, and singular work, the unique and perfect name." Emphasizing the notion of naming and the parallel of power granted to the individual, Woolf's titular choice "Mrs. Dalloway," acts as a grant of authority for Clarissa, yet also as confinement within the shadow of Robert Dalloway's identity, as socially, culturally, and politically recognized and expressed through his namesake. The introductory events to Robert Dalloway from Peter Walsh’s consciousness—spanning from page 61 to 67—the subsequent marital relationship, as well as the instability of Clarissa's sense of self and instinct, reveal an intention of subversion that does more than critique gendered knowing: they affirm Woolf’s modernist dedication to dismantling patriarchal structures, situating her with profound literary purpose among her male contemporaries.

To my ends of use for Haraway's definition of phallogocentrism, I attach the persistent androcentric framing of women as the "second sex" (e.g., Simone de Beauvoir), a hierarchical positioning that presumes women’s inferiority within the realms of instinct, logic, and intellectual authority. Woolf’s modernist prose in "Mrs. Dalloway" becomes a narrative that operates on two levels: first, as a psychological exploration of Clarissa’s own layered self-awareness, and second, as a pointed critique of the epistemological and social structures that define, limit, and obscure female experience. Woolf captures this duality with the austere of parallel protagonists, Clarissa Dalloway and Septimus Warren Smith—two beings whose paths never cross in life, yet whose experiences resonate profoundly in (symbolic) death. Through the parallel, yet fundamentally disjointed, consciousnesses of Clarissa Dalloway and Septimus Warren Smith, Woolf delves into two distinct realms: the one, a feminine experience delicately tethered to societal conventions yet subversively maneuvering through them; the other, a fractured male psyche, reeling from the desolations of war and grounded in a bleak, scientific rationality that alienates him from social order. Though their lives remain ostensibly separate, their paths echo shared undercurrents of existential inquiry and defiance. Clarissa’s self-conception emerges through subtle acts of resistance and contemplative escapism, resonating within a philosophical and almost metaphysical subversion of tradition, while Septimus, locked in a rationalistic yet traumatized detachment, spirals into isolation, his tragic death casting its shadow over lives both intimately known, and upon those who will not speak his name. 

"She felt somehow very like him - the young man who had killed himself. She felt glad that he had done it; thrown it away while they went on living. The clock was striking" (210)

Literary scholar Deborah Guth insightfully observes that "Mrs. Dalloway" is "a tale of two self-fictions—of both author and protagonist," and under cover of Clarissa’s introspection, Woolf deflects from a full realization of her character’s self-evasion. Clarissa’s symbolic images, often infused with hidden shades of meaning, allow her both to reveal and mask her emotions, underscoring a tension between societal expectations and internal, unarticulated instinct, desire, and choice as the "second sex," positioned as a 'dime-piece' to authority and power through namesake. In this light, "Mrs. Dalloway" reads more than just a novel of "modern fiction" and love; it is Woolf's nuanced examination of how the phallogocentric foundations of her world permeate the interior, private dimensions of female identity. With each strike of the clock, every passing hour of the day, the non-linear attachment of Septimus' experiences gain emotional affectivity upon Clarissa, underscoring the restrictions placed on expression of individual experiences, but also of the assumed knowledge that comes socioculturally attached: 

"A being so differently constituted from herself, with such a command of language; able to put things as editors liked them put...upon men in deference to the mysterious accord in which they, but no woman, stood to the laws of the universe; knew how to put things; knew what was said" (122)

With her pointed commentary on submissions to the Times, Woolf situates "Mrs. Dalloway" as a subtle yet potent illumination of how external epistemological hierarchies—those wielded by institutions, publications, and society at large—intrude upon and shape the interior, and even imaginary, lives of individuals. By anchoring this intrusion in the familiar rhythms of post-Edwardian London, Woolf parallels her own life’s struggles within a male-dominated literary sphere, reflecting on the way authority imposes boundaries on what is deemed knowable or understandable along lines of essentialized sex and gender. These boundaries, often inscribed as natural distinctions between "capacity" for comprehension, form a persistent barrier to true understanding; even as Woolf’s narrative structure, with its profound subjectivity, begs to be grasped by all who hold Mrs. Dalloway within their hands.
