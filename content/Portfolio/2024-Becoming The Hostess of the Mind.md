# Becoming the Hostess of the Mind: The Feminine Flow of Knowledge 

Virginia Woolf’s “Mrs. Dalloway” stands as a landmark of modernist literature, distinctively carving out space for feminine subjectivity in a male-dominated world. With the 2021 introduction by Michael Cunningham, readers are invited to compare Woolf’s narrative style with that of her contemporary, James Joyce. Cunningham notes how Joyce’s “Ulysses” delves into consciousness through exhaustive, empirical exploration, while “Mrs. Dalloway” follows a similar "unconscious" flow, Woolf's text reveals a uniquely subjective, deeply introspective form of knowing. Woolf’s narrative prioritizes feminine perspectives, focusing on the emotional depth and fluidity of inner life. Her novel challenges the phallogocentric framework—an intellectual tradition that privileges 'rational', male-centered knowledge—by framing Clarissa Dalloway’s experiences as an equally valid, if not superior, form of “knowing.” 

“Mrs. Dalloway” is not merely a testament to the exploration of the inner landscape, but to Woolf's radical reconfiguration of knowledge itself, a (re)imagining that destabilizes the hegemony of phallogocentric, empirically driven "truths." Indeed, Woolf’s assertion that she was “the only woman in England free to write what I want to write” elucidates her sense of liberation from the narrative strictures and literary industries of her time, a freedom that allows her to construct a prose style that is less linear and conclusive than that of her male contemporaries. Woolf’s prose, fluid and autotelic, immerses readers not in events with neat conclusions but in a mosaic of interior moments, impressions, and emotions that transcend traditional narrative expectations. In “Mrs. Dalloway,” Clarissa’s sense of self is assembled through deeply personal, introspective knowledge that privileges emotional resonance, bodily consciousness, and memory. It is a knowledge emphatically devoid of the empirical fixation on absolutes, as evidenced when Clarissa reflects that “she felt very young; at the same time unspeakably aged…She knew nothing; no language, no history…she would not say of herself, I am this, I am that” (7). This reflection epitomizes Woolf’s resistance to delineating Clarissa’s identity within static or prescriptive boundaries, proposing instead a non-essentialist selfhood that emerges as a dynamic site for feminine epistemic possibility—an exploration of self and ‘other’ that stands in profound divergence from traditional phallogocentric epistemology.

In stark opposition to this feminine, introspective “knowing” stands the 'one-that-got-away', Peter Walsh, whose worldview serves as an ironic counterpoint to Clarissa’s rich inner life. Peter epitomizes the traditionally masculine, empiricist lens, imposing upon women a reductive, sentimentally nostalgic existence, as encapsulated in his remark, “for women live much more in the past than we do…They attach themselves to places; and their fathers—a woman’s always proud of her father” (61). His perspective insinuates that women are bound by paternal heritage and familial loyalty, unable to transcend the symbolic weight of their own histories. Woolf, however, uses Clarissa to overturn Peter’s assumptions, revealing a feminine agency that is deeply self-sustained, inwardly defined, and wholly untethered from historical or genealogical associations. Clarissa’s cool indifference toward Peter’s advancements and observations further underscores the fundamental divide between their modes of understanding.Through this juxtaposition, Woolf reveals that Clarissa’s identity, unlike Peter’s notion of feminine identity, is not confined to a past-oriented dependency but thrives in its introspective and emotionally resonant self-awareness of all time, all the time.

In depicting Clarissa’s unconventional desires, particularly her relationship with Sally Seton, Woolf further disrupts the phallogocentric interpretation of love and passion. This bond between Clarissa and Sally embodies a form of intimacy unmediated by patriarchal desire, a purity that eschews conventional notions of attraction. Clarissa recalls her feelings for Sally with reverence, defining them as “completely disinterested” and bearing a “quality which could only exist between women, between women just grown up” (36; emphasis added). Here, Woolf presents a radical departure from the prevailing, phallocentric associations of passion; this relationship defies the masculine discourse of desire, existing in a space Woolf characterizes as uniquely feminine—a knowing derived not from erotic or possessive inclinations but from a shared, almost sacred emotional affinity. In presenting this portrayal, Woolf positions feminine love and friendship as domains of knowledge and understanding that lie beyond the reach of patriarchal paradigms of ‘knowing’; here, Woman’s passion, desire, and instinct refuse to be rendered in a language governed by male-centered desire and control—a refusal compounded by Clarissa’s own doubts about past relationships and her evolving interpretations of passion.

Through Peter’s insistence on an empiricist, male-centered understanding of passion, Woolf further critiques the narrow, patriarchal framework of emotion. His assertion that “women…don’t know what passion is. They don’t know the meaning of it to men. Clarissa was as cold as an icicle” (89) underscores a masculinist assumption that passion must follow the patterns of male experience and expression. Peter, unable to perceive the depth of Clarissa’s emotional and spiritual life, dismisses her interiority as devoid of feeling. Yet Woolf’s narrative contradicts his perception, positioning Clarissa’s emotional responses and layered introspections as evidence of a profound and complex passion. Her desires are not performative but intimate, not designed for validation but arising organically within her. Woolf thus elevates feminine subjectivity and emotion as a valid and powerful form of knowing, one that transcends Peter’s limited, externalized, and often superficial interpretations of passion.

The exploration of self-awareness is intensified in Woolf’s depiction of Clarissa’s relationship with her own aging body, where bodily consciousness becomes a source of autonomy rather than limitation. Clarissa reflects, “But often now this body she wore…this body, with all its capacities, seemed nothing—nothing at all. She had the oddest sense of being herself invisible; unseen; unknown; there being no more marrying, no more having children” (9). Here, Woolf addresses the societal erasure of aging women, particularly those who no longer fit the paradigms of beauty or reproductive potential, critiquing the phallogocentric devaluation of women as they transcend traditional roles. In feeling her body as “nothing,” Clarissa simultaneously detaches herself from the male gaze and its pervasive expectation that a woman’s worth is corporeally and reproductively anchored. Woolf’s depiction allows Clarissa to inhabit a form of “invisibility” that is liberating, an assertion of a selfhood that exists independently of the culturally imposed roles of wife or mother, thereby rejecting the reduction of her identity to an object of phallogocentric valuation.

Ultimately, Woolf conceives a model of knowledge that inherently resists the rigidities of objective codification, grounding itself instead in a subjective interiority—an intensely personal, intimate truth that affirms the autonomy and intrinsic worth of feminine subjectivity. In doing so, Woolf disrupts the gendered hierarchies of knowledge maintained by patriarchal structures. “Mrs. Dalloway” thus emerges as a compelling critique of male-centered, empirical narratives, advancing a model of understanding that honors the complexities of feminine identity and the profound sovereignty of inner life. Clarissa Dalloway becomes, in every sense, the “hostess” of her own mind, capable of discerning truths that lie beyond the reach of conventional, phallogocentric paradigms of “knowing.”
