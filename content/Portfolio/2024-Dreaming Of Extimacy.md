# Dreaming of Extimacy in the 20th-Century: Using Feminine Narrative to (Re)envision the New Woman's Intuition in Woolf, Hall, and Mansfield

With the emergence of the Modernist literary tradition among British intellectuals at the turn of the 20th century came a profound disruption of tradition—a re/framing of causal structures and a re/imagining of emotional desires, vividly expressed through both poetry and prose. While male authors of this era championed the entropic ideal of "mak[ing] it new" (Pound, 1935), female authors were delegated within the extant systems of re/cognition and nature: their writings produced precarious confrontations with this "Modern" narrative, challenging the phallogocentric authority of knowledge underpinning avant-garde aesthetics of impulse, instinct, and desire. However, many of these women’s writings simultaneously invoked the dialogics of evolving Modernist concepts of intellect and capacity, as historicized along the lines of sexology and eugenics, then codified into phallogocentric ideology.

Modernism, driven by an emotional yearning for metaphysical "truths," despite its acknowledgment of a non-universal cognitive praxis, became a fertile space for feminine narratives to destabilize and reconfigure dominant epistemologies. Engaging with inquiries from post-structuralist and French theory, I delve into how the "self-conscious separation" (Ali, 1999) central to Modernism was—whether intentionally or inadvertently—radically re/imagined by female authors who asserted agency through intuitive and instinctive modes of knowing, particularly through semiotic engagements with the dream state, as experienced extimately by the "New Women" of Britain. Texts such as Virginia Woolf’s Mrs. Dalloway, Radclyffe Hall’s The Well of Loneliness, and the short stories of Katherine Mansfield exemplify this exercise of (anti)tradition as female authors and the re/claimation of epistemological and ontological structures within the Modernist ethos. 

While re/appropriating the linguistic sentiment crafted by their male contemporaries, these female authors catalyzed a re/imagining of knowledge, envisioning emotionally-driven, instinctive knowing as a force transcending socio-cultural constructs of sex and gender, specifically through the use of self-reflective (dis)possession, experienced as both external and internal phenomena. This process constitutes a subversion of phallogocentric ideology, reflected upon the self [Woman], as these writers navigated liminal spaces of orthodoxy—self-censoring and re/classifying what is deemed "natural" within the avant-garde tradition of temporal and emotional metaphysic experimentation—what Jean Paul Satre referred to in a separate context as the "anti-novel[roman]" in the introduction to the 1948 novel, Portrait d’un inconnu, by 20th-century Russian biological-tropisme Nathalie Sarrute. In doing so, Woolf, Hall, and Mansfield forge an (anti)tradition within the literary canon, capturing the extimate (dis)possession shared by author and protagonist alike—an experience where the boundaries between internal and external realities obscure into fragmented, dream-like expressions of jouissance. These non-linear, affective modes of expression resist the rationalized and chronological demands of connaissance, dismantling its hierarchies and exposing its inadequacies in articulating feminine truths. 

In this destabilization, Woolf, Hall, and Mansfield re/imagine emotional, instinctual, and impulsive modes of agency, embodying an autotelic flow and what Squires calls the "Modernist crux": the pursuit of "[rebirth] without a frame, beyond the parameters of history" (89). For the feminine narrative, this rebirth manifests in a state of flow that transcends empirical fixation, offering a deeper emotional and instinctual understanding of "Otherness" that defies the rigid socio-cultural constraints of identity. This state acknowledges and appropriates the "naturalized" complexities of historical epistemologies surrounding sex and gender, yet concomitantly reckons with the inescapable linear progression of time while resisting expressions of emotion as strictly temporal. Through this process, these authors re/claim agency by narrating with authority, even if one’s self-reflexivity is fraught with confusion—the author and protagonist alike flow with an intuitive and instinctive mode of feminine knowing that disrupts and re/defines the Modernist literary tradition.

# Semiotic Mimesis and the Fluidity of Feminine Emotion 

At the turn of the 20th-milieu, the extant structures of semiotic and symbolic meaning and truth were rigorously contested through experimental methodologies and disruptive narrative forms by both academics and fiction authors (eg., Henri Bergson, Carl Jung, T.S. Eliot, Ezra Pound, D.H. Lawrence, and Wallace Stevens), leading to a fundamental reconfiguration of the boundaries of language, knowledge, and modes of expression. Among these challengers, female authors such as Virginia Woolf, Radclyffe Hall, and Katherine Mansfield played a pivotal role, destabilizing the framework of phallogocentric authority by employing semiotic mimesis—a concept Julia Kristeva defines as a process that simultaneously mimics and subverts the symbolic order. Kristeva’s distinction between the symbolic—aligned with structured, rational, and linguistic systems—and the semiotic, which encompasses pre-linguistic rhythms, tones, and instinctual drives such as jouissance (as contrasted to connaissance), the transgression of regulated pleasure, became a critical tool for these writers. In their fiction, combining both prose and poetics, the semiotic resists the linearity and coherence of the symbolic, offering instead a fluid, disruptive mode of expression often linked to maternal and feminine communication (Kristeva qtd. in Butler, 205-206). This distinction becomes a critical tool for female authors who navigate and challenge patriarchal literary conventions, invoking Lacan’s extimité [extimacy] and re/appropriating the expressions of internal and external dissonance and ambiguity as a means of subversion. 

French theorist Jacques Lacan introduced the neologism "extimacy" (1959–1960) derived from Freudian linguistics, merging the concepts of meta/subject-psychic exteriority and interiority to render the dynamic interplay between the self and the external world. Positioning the subject as both shaped by and constitutive of external structures, where the internal self is continuously negotiated through the fluid exchanges between external encounters and internal reflexivity, destabilizes temporally fixed notions of identity and selfhood. Central to this theory is the Lacanian conception of the Real, a domain beyond symbolic representation, where meaning remains elusive and fragmented. The Real, in its resistance to "signification" parallels Kristeva’s semiotic realm, where the pre-linguistic—defined in my case as the autotelic flow state adopted by these feminine authors—alongside feminine rhythms disrupt the coherence of the symbolic order. Through this lens, extimacy becomes a mechanism by which semiotic mimesis bridges emotional intuition with an external representation, whether through expression or environmental aesthetic/metaphor, exposing the internal self to visibility while resisting a fixed interpretation. Lacan’s controversial claim in Le Séminaire. Livre XXII (1974-75) that "the Woman does not exist" underscores that feminine experience functions as an ex-centric symptom within the dominant phallogocentric framework, existing primarily as an absence or lack within the symbolic order. Under this contextualization, feminine narratives and expressions become epistemologically conditioned by their marginal positioning, yet it is precisely within this liminality that female authors subvert dominant modes of representation. By navigating the semiotic and symbolic realms, these writers transform extimacy into a tool of empowerment, allowing the feminine self to articulate itself not as an absence, but as a destabilizing force that re/defines the boundaries of narrative, identity, and emotional knowing. 

This raises a critical question: do feminine narratives—those emerging from a creative flow state function as a product of, or as resistance to, the Modernist tradition of "making it new?" Woolf, Hall, and Mansfield position their female protagonists within this charged terrain, where subordination is intertwined with subversion. These characters traverse emotional, instinctual, and intellectual domains that defy phallogocentric epistemologies: Classical frameworks, as articulated by Aristotle and Nietzsche, dismissed feminine knowledge as subjective and relegated solely to the "aesthetic," denying its legitimacy within the structures of connaissance. Lorraine Code critiques this tradition, arguing that male dominance in knowledge systems limits women’s epistemic creativity, shaping not only what is considered knowable, but also who is recognized as capable of knowing (274). Similarly, Marianne DeKoven asserts that frames of knowledge have long been inaccessible to women, not due to cognitive inadequacy, but because of restrictive societal boundaries (268). Within this context, what constitutes self-knowledge in these narratives? How do they illustrate instinctual knowing that resists the epistemic filters imposed on early 20th-century female authors? Woolf, Hall, and Mansfield situate feminine expression at the blurred boundary of extimacy—where inner emotional realities and the outer social structures collapse into one another. Do their representations constitute authentic feminine agency, or are they semiotic mimicries shaped by phallogocentric systems? These questions form the crux of the Modernist feminine narrative, exposing the complex epistemic entanglement between knowledge, agency, and the structures that mediate them. 

For instance, Virginia Woolf’s Mrs. Dalloway exemplifies the semiotic through its fragmented, stream-of-consciousness narrative, weaving Clarissa’s memories and impressions with rhythmic shifts that privilege affective over rational knowledge. Similarly, Katherine Mansfield’s "Bliss" conveys the semiotic through Bertha’s euphoric connection to the pear tree, symbolizing instinctual fulfillment beyond articulation, yet disrupted by betrayal. In Radclyffe Hall’s The Well of Loneliness, Stephen Gordon’s instinctive recognition of her queerness exposes the limitations of heteronormative systems, embodying Kristeva’s assertion that the semiotic disrupts symbolic coherence.

# The Dialogics of Modernist Intellect and Capacity 

As Modernist realism and radicalism swept through the ranks of early 20th-century male academics, an ironic tension emerged in the project of "breaking conventionality." Male authors embraced experimentalist writing, but this innovation was often cast in "overwhelmingly male terms," rooted in phallogocentric authorship that appropriated the feminine to disrupt logocentric traditions (DeKoven qtd. in Friedman, 864). By selectively collaborating with Victorian ideals, both male and female Modernists frequently deployed domestic feminism to counterbalance radical feminist ideologies, creating the appearance of progressive feminine agency, while concomitantly safeguarding patriarchal norms (Walls 232). Elizabeth MacLeod Walls argues that this idealized image of the New Woman allowed Modernism to simultaneously accommodate feminist voices whilst re/asserting phallogocentric control, constructing a narrative that preserved male-centered authority amidst the evolving feminine integration into the ethos (241).  For example, D.H. Lawrence nostalgically portrayed an idealized feminine "flow," lamenting the modern woman as fragmented and "pointed" rather than "harmonious" (238). Post-structuralist theorists such as Derrida, Kristeva, Cixous, and Irigaray have pointed to (male) Modernist experimentalism as "an overthrow of ‘realism’" (Friedman 866). 

The focus here shifts from the dismantling of realism to how Modernist feminine narratives re/engage with it, not as a structure of empirical representation, but as a site for emotional impulse, instinct, and desire—mediated through subjective experience. In disassembling the communal reality of ‘truth,’ Modernist fiction situates its protagonists within the unstable spaces of negotiation, where "real life" is filtered through (un/conscious) transgressions of traditionality. These transgressions both illuminate the epistemic limits of knowledge and its alignment with the Modernist pursuit of affective "metaphysical truth." Jayne E. Marek identifies this process when enacted by female authors as the disestablishment of conventional causality and structure itself: a process through which subjective perception and emotional resonance supersede empirical representations of truth (5). In this destabilization, the Modernist feminine narrative raises profound questions, as the figure of the "New Woman" remains bound by phallogocentric dialogics. Transgressions that belong uniquely to the female author are rendered complex and often obscured by their position within a male-dominated framework of epistemic authority. When emotion, instinct, and desire are the central tenets of their narratives, do these expressions challenge or conform to the symbolic structures they ostensibly critique? Such questions are not merely about representation but probe the deeper fissures in Modernist epistemologies—spaces where the female author operates in a constant tension between compliance and critique. DeKoven terms this dynamic as the "gender-based dialectic," insisting that the theorization of emotion must grapple with "a multitude of antirational and unconventional forms that are incomprehensible" (15). These incomprehensible forms, she argues, can be made comprehensible only through the superlative performances of both author and protagonist—narratives that simultaneously inhabit and subvert the structures they interrogate.

# (Re)Claiming Knowledge and (Re)Envisioning Intuition

With these theoretical underpinnings, the works of Virginia Woolf, Radclyffe Hall, and Katherine Mansfield emerge as explicitly feminine texts that engage with the extimate dimensions of impulse, instinct, and desire, each articulated through distinct literary techniques. Saturated with emotional experiences of jouissance, these narratives remain absent from the designation of authoring connaissance, a term French theorists ascribed to male contemporaries like Joyce, Faulkner, and Eliot. Connaissance embodies structured, rational knowledge aligned with the symbolic order and its phallocentric privileging of hierarchy, coherence, and universality. In contrast, intuitive and emotional knowing—historically coded as feminine—has been dismissed as irrational, aligning with Aristotelian and Nietzschean reductions of the feminine to the periphery of Modernist "metaphysical truth." This paradox extends to early 20th-century feminist movements, where many Suffragists appropriated connaissance to advance their causes. Kathleen Frederickson identifies this as the impetus model, exemplified in Christabel Pankhurst’s framing of the "great scourge" to reposition instinct as rational and socially productive, akin to masculinity (125). By juxtaposing British militarism with motherhood ideals, Frederickson reveals how Suffragists invoked "reason within instinct" to subtly re/configure gendered citizenship, contesting patriarchal epistemologies while re/inforcing domestic associations (123).

This strategic re/appropriation of connaissance parallels the literary works of Woolf, Hall, and Mansfield, where protagonists seek to define a "New Woman" within fictive worlds that mirror socio-political realities. Their characters navigate societal constraints while asserting agency through desire and emotional truth. The richness of jouissance, as expressed through a dream or flow state in these texts destabilizes rigid binaries—rationality versus emotion, structure versus instinct—revealing the inadequacies of phallocentric systems in accounting for feminine subjectivity. These protagonists exist in a liminal space where critique and mimicry intertwine, resisting reductive categorization while re/defining Modernist literature’s epistemic possibilities.
